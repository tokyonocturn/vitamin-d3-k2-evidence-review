# Vitamin D3 and Vitamin K2: A Comprehensive Evidence Review

**Kevin Edwards** · November 2026 · Version 1.0.0

---

## 1. Summary

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) and vitamin K2 (menaquinone) are fat-soluble
vitamins with real physiological roles. Both are widely sold, heavily marketed,
and frequently combined in a single capsule on the theory that D3 raises calcium
absorption while K2 directs that calcium into bone instead of arteries.

That theory is mechanistically coherent and clinically unproven.

The evidence supports a narrow, useful role for vitamin D3: preventing and
treating deficiency, rickets, and osteomalacia; supporting calcium absorption;
and probably producing small reductions in cancer mortality, autoimmune disease
during treatment, and falls at modest daily doses. It does not support vitamin D
as a general-purpose preventive for fractures, cancer, heart disease, or
infection in people who are already replete. The largest randomised trials —
VITAL, D-Health, DO-HEALTH, and D2d — were designed to settle those questions,
and they largely returned null results.

Vitamin K2 has a genuine biochemical function: it is the cofactor that
carboxylates osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein, and supplementation reliably
lowers the inactive form of MGP in blood [R18; R30]. Whether that translates into
better bones or cleaner arteries is where the evidence stalls. Bone density trials
disagree with each other. Vascular calcification trials are mostly null. No trial
has been powered to detect fractures or cardiac events.

Combining the two is the weakest link in the chain. The trials that tested D3 plus
K2 directly either found no additional benefit once calcium and vitamin D were
adequate [R24; R25], or used an activated vitamin D analogue that is a prescription
drug rather than a supplement [R28]. The combination is a reasonable product to
sell. It is not an evidence-based one.

The practical conclusions are unglamorous: most people who choose to supplement
need 800–2000 IU of D3 per day, taken daily rather than in boluses, from a product
whose label accuracy is independently verified. Most do not need K2. Nobody on
warfarin should take K2 without their anticoagulation team.

## 2. What the two vitamins actually do

### 2.1 Vitamin D3

Vitamin D is a secosteroid hormone precursor, not a vitamin in the classical
sense. During UVB exposure (roughly 290–315 nm), 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin
converts to previtamin D3 and then to cholecalciferol. Cholecalciferol binds
vitamin D-binding protein and travels to the liver, where CYP2R1 adds a hydroxyl
group to form 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] — the circulating form that is
measured in blood and that has a half-life of roughly two to three weeks [R40].

A second hydroxylation, by CYP27B1 in the kidney (and in macrophages, placenta,
and other tissues), produces 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D — calcitriol — the active
hormone. Calcitriol binds the vitamin D receptor, a nuclear transcription factor
that regulates hundreds of genes, and drives intestinal calcium and phosphate
absorption, renal calcium reabsorption, and bone mineralisation [R01; R40].

This cascade explains both the established benefits and the limits. When 25(OH)D
is very low, calcium absorption falls, parathyroid hormone rises, and bone is
demineralised — the pathway behind rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults.
When 25(OH)D is adequate, adding more does not proportionally increase absorption
or bone density; the system is already supplied.

### 2.2 Vitamin K2

Vitamin K is a family of naphthoquinones defined by a shared ability to support
γ-glutamyl carboxylation. That reaction converts specific glutamate residues in
target proteins into γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla), which binds calcium.

- **Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone)** comes from leafy green vegetables and is the
  main dietary form. It is preferentially taken up by the liver and supports
  clotting factor synthesis.
- **Vitamin K2 (menaquinones)** share a unsaturated isoprenoid side chain of
  variable length. **MK-4** is short-chain, present in animal products, and
  cleared within about an hour. **MK-7** is long-chain, produced by fermentation
  (notably *Bacillus subtilis natto*), and has a half-life of about 68–72 hours
  [R59; R38].

Two K-dependent proteins matter here:

- **Osteocalcin**, made by osteoblasts, must be carboxylated to bind
  hydroxyapatite. Undercarboxylated osteocalcin is a marker of poor vitamin K
  status and is routinely used as a surrogate endpoint in K2 trials.
- **Matrix Gla protein (MGP)**, made by vascular smooth muscle cells, inhibits
  vascular calcification. Its inactive, dephosphorylated-uncarboxylated form
  (dp-ucMGP) falls reliably when MK-7 is supplemented [R30; R36].

The mechanistic case for K2 is therefore stronger than for most supplements: it
does something measurable to proteins that plausibly matter. The clinical case is
what fails to follow.

### 2.3 The proposed synergy

Vitamin D increases calcium absorption and induces osteocalcin expression.
Vitamin K2 is needed to carboxylate osteocalcin and MGP. From those two facts,
marketers infer that D3 without K2 may deposit calcium in arteries and that K2
"directs calcium to bone."

This is a hypothesis, not a finding. It requires that (a) D3 supplementation
measurably raises uncarboxylated osteocalcin in people with adequate vitamin K,
(b) K2 supplementation reduces vascular calcification or fractures, and (c) the
combination outperforms D3 alone on a hard outcome. Only the first has partial
support, and even that is inconsistent.

## 3. Forms, absorption, and bioavailability

### 3.1 Vitamin D forms

| Form | Source | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **D3 (cholecalciferol)** | Lanolin from sheep wool, or lichen for vegan products | The form used in almost all trials; raises 25(OH)D more than D2 |
| **D2 (ergocalciferol)** | UV-irradiated yeast ergosterol | Prescription high-dose products in the US; less potent at raising total 25(OH)D |
| **25(OH)D3 (calcifediol)** | Synthetic; prescription in some countries | Three to five times as potent per microgram; not a typical retail supplement |
| **Alfacalcidol / calcitriol** | Synthetic activated analogues | Prescription drugs; not interchangeable with cholecalciferol |

The D2-versus-D3 question is settled. A 2024 meta-analysis of 20 comparative
studies found that daily D2 produced total 25(OH)D about 10.39 nmol/L (40%) lower
than daily D3 at the same dose, analysed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass
spectrometry [R43]. The difference was attenuated in people with BMI above 25,
which is itself informative: adiposity dilutes the response to either form.

The clinical implication is simple: choose D3 unless a clinician has specifically
prescribed D2 or an activated analogue.

### 3.2 Vitamin K forms

| Form | Typical retail dose | Half-life | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **K1 (phylloquinone)** | 90–120 mcg (as dietary intake) | 1–2 hours | Liver-preferential; the form most relevant to clotting |
| **MK-4** | 45 mcg–15 mg | About 1 hour | Does not accumulate in serum on repeated dosing [R44]; Japanese trials used 45 mg/day |
| **MK-7** | 45–720 mcg | 68–72 hours | Accumulates to steady state over about two weeks; 2.5–6× the bioavailability of K1 [R59] |

MK-4 and MK-7 are not interchangeable by mass. A 420 mcg dose of MK-4 was
undetectable in serum at every time point in a direct comparison, while the same
dose of MK-7 peaked at six hours and remained detectable at 48 hours [R44]. This
is why MK-4 products use milligram doses and MK-7 products use microgram doses,
and why comparing them on price per milligram is meaningless.

A practical quality issue specific to K2: the active form is *all-trans* MK-7.
Chemical synthesis produces cis isomers that lack vitamin K activity. A 2018
analysis of marketed products found all-trans content often below the label
declaration, with cis/trans MK-6 and MK-7 impurities ranging from about 71 to
219 mcg per tablet or capsule, and a pattern where tablets under-delivered while
oil capsules over-delivered [R45]. Fermented MK-7 (as in MenaQ7) avoids the
synthetic isomer problem.

### 3.3 Absorption

Vitamin D is absorbed in the small intestine and incorporated into chylomicrons,
so it requires fat and bile. The evidence on meal effects is modest but
consistent in direction:

- A crossover study found peak plasma vitamin D3 32% higher (95% CI 11–52%) when
  a 50,000 IU dose was taken with a fat-containing meal rather than a fat-free
  one [R41].
- A separate trial found higher absorption with a low-fat meal than with a
  high-fat meal or no meal, but **no difference in 25(OH)D at 30 or 90 days**
  [R42]. In other words, the acute pharmacokinetic effect does not reliably
  translate into a different steady-state level.

The practical rule is to take D3 with a meal containing some fat, because it is
free to do so and may help marginally. Do not expect a large difference, and do
not pay a premium for "enhanced absorption" claims that have no 25(OH)D data
behind them. There is no controlled evidence that sprays, sublingual drops, or
micellised formulations produce a better 25(OH)D response than a standard softgel
taken with food.

One exception deserves mention: in people with fat malabsorption (cystic
fibrosis, pancreatic insufficiency, bariatric surgery, cholestatic liver disease),
absorption is impaired regardless of vehicle, and higher doses under specialist
supervision are usually required [R02; R40].

## 4. Bone health

### 4.1 What is established

Vitamin D prevents and treats **rickets** and **osteomalacia**. This is not
controversial and is the basis for infant supplementation and for fortification
programmes. The mechanism is direct: without adequate 25(OH)D, intestinal calcium
absorption falls below what mineralisation requires.

Beyond deficiency, the picture narrows sharply.

### 4.2 Fractures in replete adults: no benefit

VITAL randomised 25,871 adults aged 50 and older (men) or 55 and older (women) to
2000 IU vitamin D3 daily or placebo for a median of 5.3 years. In the fracture
ancillary study, total fractures occurred in 769 of 12,927 participants on
vitamin D and 782 of 12,944 on placebo — **HR 0.98 (95% CI 0.89–1.08)**. Hip
fractures: **HR 1.01 (0.70–1.47)**. There was no effect modification by age, sex,
race, BMI, or baseline 25(OH)D, including in participants below 12 ng/mL, although
that subgroup was small [R06].

D-Health, which gave 60,000 IU monthly to 21,315 Australians aged 60–84, found
**HR 0.94 (0.84–1.06)** for total fractures over five years [R10]. The 2026 BMJ
umbrella review of 69 trials and 153,902 participants found vitamin D alone had
**no effect on any fracture (RR 1.00, 0.95–1.06)** and that even combined calcium
plus vitamin D produced absolute reductions below thresholds considered clinically
meaningful [R21].

### 4.3 Where calcium plus vitamin D does help

The exception is older, frailer, often institutionalised populations with low
calcium and vitamin D intake. The Cochrane review found calcium plus vitamin D
reduced hip fracture **RR 0.84 (0.74–0.96)** across nine trials and 49,853
participants, while vitamin D alone did not (RR 1.12 for hip fracture) [R19]. An
umbrella review reached the same conclusion: benefit is concentrated in
institutionalised individuals and is not seen in trials restricted to
community-dwelling adults [R20].

This distinction — deficiency correction versus supplementation of the replete —
resolves most of the apparent contradiction in the literature. Both bodies of
evidence are correct; they describe different populations.

### 4.4 Falls: a dose-shaped curve

Falls are where dose matters most. A 2024 network meta-analysis of 35 trials and
58,937 participants found **800–1000 IU/day reduced falls (RR 0.85, 0.74–0.95)**,
while doses above 1000 IU/day were associated with *more* falls, and intermittent
dosing showed no benefit [R61].

The harm signal is real. Sanders and colleagues gave 2256 women aged 70 and older
a single annual oral dose of 500,000 IU. Falls increased (**IRR 1.15, 1.02–1.30**)
and fractures increased (**IRR 1.26, 1.00–1.59**), with the excess concentrated in
the first three months [R17]. The US Preventive Services Task Force consequently
recommends against vitamin D supplementation to prevent falls in community-dwelling
older adults [R18].

The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline reflects this by preferring daily, lower-dose
supplementation over non-daily, higher doses in every population where it suggests
empiric vitamin D [R02].

### 4.5 Vitamin K2 and bone

The K2 bone literature is small, heterogeneous, and inconsistent.

**Positive trials.**

- Knapen et al. gave 244 healthy postmenopausal women 180 mcg MK-7 or placebo for
  three years. MK-7 improved vitamin K status and reduced the age-related decline
  in bone mineral density and content at the lumbar spine and femoral neck, but
  not at the total hip, and reduced vertebral height loss [R23].
- A Chinese trial of 311 adults aged 50–75 found 90 mcg MK-7/day reduced femoral
  neck bone loss in postmenopausal women over one year, but not in men. Adding
  calcium and vitamin D3 produced **no additional effect** [R25].
- Japanese trials of MK-4 at 45 mg/day — a prescription-level dose — reported
  fewer clinical fractures in osteoporosis, but were largely open-label and
  conducted in a regulatory context where MK-4 is an approved osteoporosis drug
  [R29].

**Null trials.**

- Rønn et al. randomised 142 postmenopausal women with osteopenia to 375 mcg
  MK-7 or placebo for three years, with **both groups** receiving vitamin D3
  (38 mcg/day) and calcium (800 mg/day). Osteocalcin carboxylation improved
  substantially, but areal BMD declined at every site with **no between-group
  difference** (p > 0.09) [R24]. This is the best-powered direct test of the
  "K2 adds to D3 and calcium" hypothesis, and it was negative.
- Trevasc-HDK found no effect of K2 on vascular calcification in haemodialysis
  patients [R32], and AVADEC found no effect on aortic valve calcification despite
  a large reduction in dp-ucMGP [R30].

**Meta-analyses.**

- Mott et al. found vitamin K reduced any clinical fracture in postmenopausal or
  osteoporotic patients (**OR 0.72, 0.55–0.95**), but the estimate weakened to
  **0.76 (0.58–1.01)** when restricted to low-risk-of-bias trials, and there was
  no difference in vertebral fractures. BMD differences were small and not robust
  to risk-of-bias adjustment [R26].
- A 2022 meta-analysis of 16 RCTs and 6425 participants found improved lumbar
  spine BMD but **no significant difference in fracture incidence (RR 0.96)**
  [R27].

The honest summary: K2 may slow bone loss modestly in some populations, probably
via osteocalcin carboxylation, but the effect is small, inconsistent, not clearly
additive to vitamin D and calcium, and unproven for fracture prevention.

## 5. Calcium regulation and cardiovascular claims

### 5.1 The mechanism

MGP is secreted by vascular smooth muscle cells and is one of the strongest
endogenous inhibitors of arterial calcification. It requires vitamin K-dependent
carboxylation to function. In population studies, low vitamin K status and high
dp-ucMGP are associated with vascular calcification and mortality [R58].

MK-7 supplementation reliably and substantially lowers dp-ucMGP — in AVADEC,
by about 212 pmol/L versus a 45 pmol/L rise on placebo (p < 0.001) [R30]. This is
one of the most reproducible biomarker effects in supplement research.

### 5.2 What the outcome trials show

| Trial | Population | Intervention | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **AVADEC** (2022) | 365 men aged 65–74 with aortic valve calcification >300 AU | MK-7 720 mcg + D3 25 mcg daily, 2 years | Aortic valve calcium progressed equally: mean difference 17 AU (95% CI −86 to 53; p=0.64) [R30] |
| **AVADEC CAC substudy** (2023) | 304 of the same men | Same | CAC rose 203 vs 254 AU (p=0.089); in those with CAC ≥400 AU, 288 vs 380 AU (p=0.047) [R31] |
| **Trevasc-HDK** (2023) | 178 haemodialysis patients | K2, 18 months | No difference in coronary or aortic calcification, arterial stiffness, or major events [R32] |
| **Shea et al.** (2009) | 388 older adults | Vitamin K1 500 mcg, 3 years | No significant attenuation of CAC progression overall [R34] |
| **Meta-analysis** (2023) | Pooled RCTs | Various K forms | CAC mean difference −17.37 AU (95% CI −34.18 to −0.56) [R36] |

The pattern is consistent: a small surrogate signal in pooled analyses, driven by
smaller trials, with the largest and most rigorous dedicated trials neutral. No
trial has been powered for myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death.
The observational Rotterdam data linking higher K2 intake to lower coronary heart
disease is hypothesis-generating, not causal [R58].

A 2026 review of the field summarises the position well: the mechanistic
foundations are strong, the clinical evidence is conflicting, and the unresolved
questions are dose, duration, population, and whether surrogate changes matter
[R33].

### 5.3 Vitamin D and cardiovascular disease

Vitamin D receptor is expressed in vascular tissue and cardiomyocytes, and
observational studies link low 25(OH)D to cardiovascular events. Randomisation
does not confirm causation.

- VITAL: major cardiovascular events **HR 0.97 (0.85–1.12)** over 5.3 years [R05].
- D-Health: major cardiovascular events **HR 0.91 (0.81–1.01)**, with a possible
  benefit in participants already taking cardiovascular drugs (0.84, 0.74–0.97),
  a subgroup finding that did not survive interaction testing [R11].

A reasonable reading is that vitamin D neither causes nor prevents cardiovascular
disease at supplemental doses, and that any effect is small enough to require
very large trials to detect.

### 5.4 The calcium question

One legitimate concern about high-dose vitamin D is that raising calcium
absorption could, in theory, increase vascular or renal calcium load. The trials
do not support a clinically important effect at ordinary doses: VITAL found no
increase in cardiovascular events or kidney stones, and calcification trials that
added D3 to K2 found no acceleration attributable to D3 [R05; R30]. The signal
that does exist belongs to calcium supplementation, not vitamin D — the WHI
calcium-plus-D arm reported more kidney stones, and calcium supplements have been
associated with gastrointestinal and possibly cardiovascular harms in
meta-analyses [R40].

## 6. Immune function

### 6.1 Respiratory infections

The vitamin D receptor is expressed in immune cells, and calcitriol induces
cathelicidin and β-defensin. The trial literature has moved substantially.

- **2017 individual-participant meta-analysis:** 25 trials, 11,321 participants,
  **OR 0.88 (0.81–0.96)** for at least one acute respiratory infection. Daily or
  weekly dosing without bolus gave **OR 0.81 (0.72–0.91)**; bolus dosing gave
  0.97. The effect was much larger in participants with baseline 25(OH)D below
  25 nmol/L (OR 0.30) [R13].
- **2025 aggregate meta-analysis:** 40 studies, 61,589 participants,
  **OR 0.94 (0.88–1.00), p=0.057**, with no subgroup effect modification by age,
  baseline status, dosing frequency, or dose, and funnel-plot asymmetry
  suggesting publication bias [R14].
- **2024 dose–response analysis:** no overall effect (RR 0.99), but daily dosing
  RR 0.92 and winter-dominant settings RR 0.79, with a number needed to treat
  around 10 in winter [R62].

The most defensible position is that daily low-dose vitamin D produces a small
reduction in respiratory infections, mainly in people with low baseline status and
mainly in winter, and that this effect is smaller than the early literature
suggested. It is not a treatment for established infection, and it is not a
substitute for vaccination.

### 6.2 Autoimmune disease

VITAL's autoimmune ancillary was one of the more striking findings in the field.
Over 5.3 years, vitamin D 2000 IU/day reduced confirmed incident autoimmune
disease by 22%: **HR 0.78 (0.61–0.99)**, with similar effects for vitamin D alone
and combined with omega-3 [R07].

The follow-up matters as much as the result. Two years after supplementation
stopped, the cumulative hazard ratio was **0.98 (0.83–1.17)** — the benefit had
disappeared [R08]. This suggests a treatment effect during exposure rather than a
permanent modification of disease risk, and it argues against framing vitamin D as
a lifelong autoimmune preventive.

### 6.3 COVID-19

Observational associations between low 25(OH)D and severe COVID-19 generated
enormous interest. Randomised evidence has not supported a causal preventive
effect, and the largest synthesis of respiratory infection trials is null overall
[R14; R22]. Vitamin D status is a marker of frailty and ill health as much as a
cause of it.

## 7. Cancer and mortality

### 7.1 Cancer incidence: no

VITAL found invasive cancer in 793 participants on vitamin D and 824 on placebo
over 5.3 years: **HR 0.96 (0.88–1.06)** [R05]. The 2019 meta-analysis of 10 trials
found no reduction in total cancer incidence (**RR 0.98, 0.93–1.03**) [R16].

### 7.2 Cancer mortality: a small, real signal

The same meta-analysis found **cancer mortality RR 0.87 (0.79–0.96)** across five
trials and 1,591 cancer deaths, largely attributable to trials using daily dosing
rather than infrequent boluses [R16].
A separate 50-trial analysis of 74,655 participants found cancer death **RR 0.85
(0.74–0.97)**, while all-cause mortality was unchanged (RR 0.98) [R15].

This is a genuine but small effect. In absolute terms, a 13–15% relative reduction
in cancer mortality corresponds to a few fewer deaths per thousand person-years,
and it is not a reason for a healthy 30-year-old to take vitamin D. It is a
reasonable secondary argument for supplementation in older adults.

### 7.3 All-cause mortality

The pooled estimate across 50 trials is **RR 0.98 (0.95–1.02)** — no overall
effect [R15]. D-Health, the largest single trial of monthly dosing, found
**HR 1.04 (0.93–1.18)** [R09].

The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline nevertheless suggests empiric vitamin D for
adults aged 75 and older, based on a very small mortality reduction with high
certainty in that subgroup, and explicitly prefers daily low-dose over
non-daily high-dose supplementation [R02; R03].

### 7.4 The 75-plus exception

This is one of the few places where a guideline recommends supplementation for a
whole population without testing. The reasoning is that the absolute benefit is
small but the intervention is cheap and safe, and that older adults have the
highest prevalence of low status and the highest baseline mortality risk. It is a
defensible public-health judgement, not a strong individual-level effect.

## 8. Glycaemic outcomes

The D2d trial randomised 2423 adults with prediabetes to 4000 IU/day or placebo
for a median of 2.5 years. New-onset diabetes occurred in 293 versus 323
participants: **HR 0.88 (0.75–1.04), p=0.12** [R12].

Pooling D2d with the Norwegian Tromsø and Japanese DPVD trials — all designed to
test this question — produced a **15% reduction in progression to diabetes** and a
**30% increase in regression to normal glucose regulation** [R04]. Each trial was
underpowered individually; the pooled signal is consistent in direction.

The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests empiric vitamin D for adults with
high-risk prediabetes to reduce progression, alongside lifestyle modification
[R02]. This is a modest adjunct, not a substitute for weight loss, diet, or
metformin.

## 9. Blood testing and deficiency thresholds

### 9.1 What the test measures

Serum **25-hydroxyvitamin D** is the standard test. It reflects total body vitamin
D from skin and diet, has a two-to-three-week half-life, and is stable enough for
clinical use. It does not measure the active hormone, and it should not be used to
diagnose hypercalcemia or sarcoidosis.

Units are ng/mL or nmol/L; multiply ng/mL by 2.496 to get nmol/L. A level of
30 ng/mL is 75 nmol/L; 20 ng/mL is 50 nmol/L; 12 ng/mL is 30 nmol/L.

Assay variability is a real problem: immunoassays and mass spectrometry can differ
by 10–20%, and results from different laboratories are not always interchangeable.
For individual decision-making, trends within one laboratory are more useful than
absolute values across laboratories.

### 9.2 The threshold debate

| Body | Year | Position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Institute of Medicine / NASEM** | 2011 | 25(OH)D ≥20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) meets the needs of about 97.5% of the population; <12 ng/mL is at risk of deficiency; 12–20 ng/mL is at risk of inadequacy [R01] |
| **Endocrine Society** | 2011 | ≥30 ng/mL sufficient; 21–29 insufficient; <20 deficient (applied to at-risk patients, not the general population) |
| **Endocrine Society** | 2024 | No outcome-based threshold can be specified; the 30 ng/mL target is dropped; routine screening is not recommended in any general-population group considered [R02; R64] |

The 2024 guideline is a genuine change of position, not a refinement. Its panel
reviewed the trial evidence and concluded that no 25(OH)D level has been shown to
predict a benefit from supplementation in healthy people, and that testing
therefore does not change management for most people [R02; R03].

### 9.3 When testing is appropriate

Testing remains appropriate when the result will change management:

- suspected rickets or osteomalacia;
- malabsorption (coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, cystic fibrosis,
  bariatric surgery, short bowel);
- chronic kidney disease, including dialysis;
- hypocalcaemia or unexplained bone pain;
- granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, tuberculosis) or lymphoma, where endogenous
  calcitriol production can cause hypercalcemia;
- primary hyperparathyroidism;
- medications that accelerate vitamin D catabolism (anticonvulsants, some
  antiretrovirals, glucocorticoids);
- osteoporosis treatment, where calcium and vitamin D status must be corrected
  before antiresorptives;
- pregnancy in women with additional risk factors;
- dark skin plus limited sun exposure plus symptoms, on clinical judgement;
- before and after high-dose correction.

The 2024 guideline explicitly excludes these from its "do not test" advice; it
addresses generally healthy people without established indications [R02].

### 9.4 Interpreting a result

| 25(OH)D | ng/mL | Interpretation | Typical action |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| <12 | <30 | Deficient; risk of rickets/osteomalacia | Correction course, clinician involvement |
| 12–20 | 30–50 | At risk of inadequacy | 800–2000 IU/day, recheck in 3 months |
| 20–50 | 50–125 | Sufficient for almost everyone | 600–1000 IU/day maintenance if desired |
| 50–100 | 125–250 | High; no proven benefit | Reduce dose; avoid bolus |
| >150 | >375 | Potentially toxic | Stop supplement; check calcium; clinician |

Note that the upper end is not a target. The 2024 guideline found no evidence that
higher levels are better, and NHANES data show the proportion of Americans above
125 nmol/L rising from 2.2% to 7.6% between 2007 and 2023 [R56].

## 10. Recommended intake, upper limits, and toxicity

### 10.1 Recommended dietary allowances

| Group | RDA (IOM) | Supplemental range used in practice |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Infants 0–12 months | 400 IU | 400 IU |
| Children 1–18 years | 600 IU | 600–1000 IU; 300–2000 IU in the 2024 guideline's trial range |
| Adults 19–70 years | 600 IU | 600–2000 IU |
| Adults 71+ | 800 IU | 800–2000 IU |
| Pregnancy/lactation | 600 IU | 600–2000 IU, not above the upper limit |
| Adults 75+ (2024 guideline) | — | 400–3333 IU/day, average trial dose about 900 IU |

The RDA assumes minimal sun exposure. People with regular sun exposure may need
less; people with dark skin, obesity, or limited outdoor activity may need more.

### 10.2 Upper limits

The tolerable upper intake level for adults is **4000 IU/day (100 mcg)** [R01].
For children: 1000 IU (0–6 months), 1500 IU (6–12 months), 2500 IU (1–3 years),
3000 IU (4–8 years). The European Food Safety Authority uses the same adult value.

The 2024 Endocrine Society did not raise the upper limit, and its recommendations
stay within it. Higher doses are used clinically to correct deficiency, but under
monitoring rather than as maintenance.

### 10.3 Toxicity and hypercalcemia

Vitamin D toxicity is caused by excessive intake, not by sun exposure. It
produces hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria, and in severe cases nephrocalcinosis,
renal failure, soft-tissue calcification, arrhythmia, and death [R40].

Key thresholds:

- Toxicity is **rare below 25(OH)D of 150 ng/mL (375 nmol/L)** [R40].
- Sustained intakes **above 10,000 IU/day** are the usual cause; toxicity from
  food alone is essentially impossible.
- A three-year trial of 400, 4000, and 10,000 IU/day in 373 healthy adults found
  mild hypercalcemia in **0%, 3%, and 9%** respectively, all resolving on repeat
  testing, with no difference in clinical adverse events [R54].
- A meta-analysis of 22 trials at 3200–4000 IU/day found hypercalcemia **RR 2.21
  (1.26–3.87)**, an absolute excess of about 4 cases per 1000 people, plus small
  increases in falls (RR 1.25) and hospitalisation (RR 1.16) [R52].

The clinical picture of toxicity is worth knowing because it is easy to miss:
nausea, vomiting, constipation, anorexia, polyuria, polydipsia, weakness,
confusion, and in severe cases arrhythmia. Any of these in someone taking high-dose
vitamin D should prompt a calcium and 25(OH)D measurement.

### 10.4 Kidney stones

The kidney stone question is frequently misstated.

- Vitamin D alone does not appear to increase stone risk. In a trial of 100,000 IU
  monthly (about 3300 IU/day) over a median of 3.3 years, the hazard ratio for a
  first stone event was **0.90 (0.66–1.23)** [R53].
- The 2024 guideline's evidence review found **RR 1.00 (0.84–1.19)** for
  nephrolithiasis with intermittent high-dose vitamin D [R02].
- Calcium supplementation is different: the WHI calcium-plus-vitamin D arm
  reported more kidney stones, and calcium loading increases urinary calcium
  [R40].

People with a history of calcium stones should not avoid vitamin D out of fear;
they should avoid unnecessary calcium supplements, stay hydrated, and discuss
dosing with a clinician.

## 11. Medication interactions

### 11.1 Vitamin K antagonists — the critical interaction

Warfarin, acenocoumarol, phenprocoumon, and tioclomarol work by blocking vitamin K
epoxide reductase, depleting the reduced vitamin K needed to carboxylate clotting
factors II, VII, IX, and X. Adding vitamin K therefore antagonises the drug.

The dose-response is steeper than most people assume. In a randomised study of 42
volunteers stabilised on acenocoumarol, MK-7 at **45 mcg/day reduced INR by about
20% after one week and 37% after two weeks** (p=0.002 and p=0.001). Doses of
10 and 20 mcg/day were independently judged by two haematologists to cause a
clinically relevant INR reduction in at least 40% and 60% of subjects [R37].

The authors' conclusion is unambiguous: **MK-7 supplements should be avoided in
patients receiving vitamin K antagonist therapy.** This applies to K1 supplements
and to multivitamins containing vitamin K, and to large, variable intakes of
natto.

Patients on warfarin do not need to avoid vitamin K entirely — they need
*consistent* intake, because the dose is titrated against it. Starting or stopping
a K2 supplement is exactly the kind of sudden change that destabilises INR.

### 11.2 Direct oral anticoagulants

Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, and dabigatran inhibit thrombin or factor Xa
directly and do not depend on vitamin K. There is no pharmacological mechanism for
vitamin K2 to reduce their effect, and no clinical trial has tested the
combination. "No known interaction" is not the same as "studied and safe," but the
mechanistic argument is strong [R39].

Patients often conflate "blood thinner" categories. The distinction matters:
K2 is contraindicated with warfarin and probably fine with apixaban.

### 11.3 Other interactions relevant to vitamin D

| Drug or class | Effect | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Thiazide diuretics** | Reduce renal calcium excretion | Additive hypercalcemia risk at high vitamin D doses; monitor calcium |
| **Digoxin** | Hypercalcemia potentiates toxicity | Keep 25(OH)D and calcium in range |
| **Anticonvulsants** (phenytoin, phenobarbital, carbamazepine, primidone) | Induce hepatic 25-hydroxylase | Lower 25(OH)D; higher maintenance doses often needed |
| **Glucocorticoids** | Reduce calcium absorption, increase bone loss | Ensure adequate D and calcium; bone protection often indicated |
| **Orlistat, cholestyramine, colestipol** | Reduce fat-soluble vitamin absorption | Separate dosing; monitor 25(OH)D |
| **Bariatric surgery** | Bypasses absorption sites | Higher doses, lifelong monitoring |
| **Ketoconazole** | Inhibits 1α-hydroxylase and 25-hydroxylase | Rare but relevant in prolonged therapy |
| **Antiretrovirals** (efavirenz, tenofovir) | Altered vitamin D metabolism | Monitor in long-term therapy |
| **Aluminium-containing antacids** | Hypercalcemia increases aluminium absorption in renal failure | Avoid in dialysis patients with hypercalcemia |

Vitamin D itself has few clinically important pharmacokinetic interactions; most of
the concern is pharmacodynamic (calcium) or disease-specific.

### 11.4 Vitamin K and antibiotics

Broad-spectrum antibiotics reduce gut bacteria that synthesise menaquinones, but
dietary K1 is the dominant source and clinically significant deficiency is rare
outside malnutrition, malabsorption, or anticoagulation. The interaction that
matters is with vitamin K antagonists, not with antibiotics alone.

## 12. Does combining D3 with K2 add meaningful benefit?

This is the central commercial question, and the evidence is weaker than the
marketing implies.

### 12.1 The direct trials

| Trial | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Iwamoto 2000** | 92 postmenopausal women with osteoporosis; four arms: alfacalcidol, MK-4 45 mg, both, or calcium; 2 years | Combined group increased lumbar BMD more than either alone [R28] |
| **Chinese 2020** | 311 adults; placebo, K2 50 mcg, K2 90 mcg, or K2 90 mcg + calcium 500 mg + D3 10 mcg; 1 year | K2 90 mcg reduced femoral neck bone loss; adding calcium and D3 gave **no additional benefit** [R25] |
| **Rønn 2020** | 142 postmenopausal women with osteopenia; MK-7 375 mcg vs placebo, both on D3 38 mcg + calcium 800 mg; 3 years | Osteocalcin carboxylation improved; **no BMD or microarchitecture difference** [R24] |
| **AVADEC 2022** | 365 men with aortic valve calcification; MK-7 720 mcg + D3 25 mcg vs placebo; 2 years | **No difference** in valve or coronary calcification [R30] |

The only positive combination trial used **alfacalcidol** (1α-hydroxyvitamin D3),
a prescription prodrug of calcitriol that bypasses 25-hydroxylation. It is not
cholecalciferol, it is not sold as a dietary supplement in most countries, and its
use requires monitoring for hypercalcemia. Citing it as evidence that "D3 + K2
builds bone" is a category error.

### 12.2 Why the hypothesis is plausible but unproven

D3 increases osteocalcin expression and calcium absorption. K2 carboxylates
osteocalcin. In theory, D3 without adequate K2 could produce undercarboxylated
osteocalcin that cannot bind mineral, and K2 could correct that. In practice:

- People eating green vegetables and dairy usually have adequate vitamin K status
  for carboxylation; the prevalence of functional deficiency in healthy adults is
  low.
- K2 trials show carboxylation improves but BMD does not, suggesting the
  carboxylation step is not rate-limiting for bone density in well-nourished
  people.
- The outcome that would matter — fracture — has never been tested in a
  D3-versus-D3+K2 trial.

### 12.3 Verdict

**Unsupported for hard outcomes.** There is no randomised evidence that adding K2
to D3 reduces fractures, cardiovascular events, or mortality. There is weak,
inconsistent evidence for a small BMD effect, and one well-powered trial that
found none [R24]. The combination is not harmful for most people, and it is not
expensive relative to many supplements — but it is not evidence-based, and the
premium over D3 alone is roughly 4.5-fold for the reference regimen (see the
[generated brand table](../docs/generated/brand-comparison.md)).

## 13. Who may benefit, and who should avoid supplementation

### 13.1 Likely to benefit

| Group | Why | Reasonable approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **People with documented deficiency** (25(OH)D <12–20 ng/mL) | Direct evidence for correction | Clinician-guided correction, then maintenance |
| **Adults 75 and older** | Small mortality reduction, high prevalence of low status | 800–1000 IU/day, daily |
| **Institutionalised or housebound older adults** | Low sun exposure, low intake, fracture risk | 800–1000 IU/day with calcium if intake is low |
| **People with malabsorption or bariatric surgery** | Reduced absorption | Higher doses, monitored |
| **People with dark skin in high latitudes** | Reduced cutaneous synthesis | 1000–2000 IU/day, especially in winter |
| **People with obesity** | Volumetric dilution, lower response | 2–3× usual dose, recheck |
| **Exclusively breastfed infants** | Milk is a poor vitamin D source | 400 IU/day |
| **People with prediabetes** | 15% relative reduction in progression | 1000–2000 IU/day plus lifestyle change |
| **People with osteoporosis on treatment** | Correct deficiency before antiresorptives | Clinician-guided |
| **People with chronic kidney disease** | Impaired activation | Nephrology-guided, including active analogues |

### 13.2 Unlikely to benefit

- Healthy adults under 50 with adequate sun exposure and diet.
- People already taking a multivitamin with 600–1000 IU.
- Athletes seeking performance gains: vitamin D does not improve performance in
  replete athletes.
- Anyone hoping to prevent cancer, heart disease, or COVID-19.
- Anyone using K2 alone for cardiovascular prevention.

### 13.3 Should avoid or seek specialist advice

| Group | Concern |
| --- | --- |
| **Anyone taking warfarin, acenocoumarol, phenprocoumon, or tioclomarol** | Vitamin K2 (and K1) antagonises anticoagulation; INR can fall substantially [R37] |
| **Hypercalcemia of any cause** | Vitamin D worsens it |
| **Sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, lymphoma, other granulomatous disease** | Extrarenal 1α-hydroxylation can cause hypercalcemia |
| **Primary hyperparathyroidism** | Calcium elevation may worsen |
| **Calcium-containing kidney stones** | Avoid calcium supplements; vitamin D with clinician oversight |
| **Chronic kidney disease stage 4–5 or dialysis** | Impaired calcium/phosphate handling; requires monitoring |
| **Pregnancy** | Do not exceed the upper limit; high-dose boluses are inappropriate |
| **Infants** | Only 400 IU under paediatric guidance; toxicity risk from overfortification |
| **People with vitamin D hypersensitivity (rare)** | Idiopathic hypercalcemia |
| **People taking thiazides or digoxin at high vitamin D doses** | Additive hypercalcemia risk |

## 14. Dosing strategies

### 14.1 Maintenance

- **600–800 IU/day** meets the RDA for most adults.
- **800–1000 IU/day** is the range associated with fewer falls and is the
  Endocrine Society's preferred style for older adults [R61; R02].
- **1000–2000 IU/day** is a reasonable choice for people with lower sun exposure,
  dark skin, obesity, or age over 75.
- **Take with a meal containing fat**, once daily, at a consistent time.

### 14.2 Correction of deficiency

Common clinical regimens (under supervision):

- 50,000 IU once weekly for 8 weeks, then 1500–2000 IU/day; or
- 6000 IU/day for 8 weeks, then 1500–2000 IU/day; or
- 2000–4000 IU/day for 12 weeks in milder insufficiency.

Recheck 25(OH)D after 8–12 weeks. Avoid single large boluses; they are associated
with falls and fracture harm and do not produce better correction [R17; R02].

### 14.3 Special situations

- **Obesity:** 2–3× the usual dose, because adipose tissue sequesters vitamin D
  and BMI blunts the 25(OH)D response [R43].
- **Malabsorption:** 3000–6000 IU/day or higher, with monitoring; powder
  formulations may absorb better than oil in pancreatic insufficiency.
- **Anticonvulsants:** higher maintenance doses, monitored.
- **Age 75+:** 800–1000 IU/day, daily, not bolus [R02].
- **Pregnancy:** 600–2000 IU/day, not exceeding 4000 IU; avoid high-dose boluses.
- **Infants:** 400 IU/day.

### 14.4 Vitamin K2 dosing, if used

- **MK-7:** 90–180 mcg/day is the range used in most trials; 180 mcg was used in
  the positive BMD trial [R23]; 375 mcg was used in the null trial [R24].
- **MK-4:** 45 mg/day is the Japanese prescription dose; retail 45 mcg–15 mg doses
  are far below the trial dose and are not equivalent to MK-7.
- **Take with a fat-containing meal**, like vitamin D.
- **Do not use with vitamin K antagonists.**

There is no established RDA for K2 specifically. The adequate intake for total
vitamin K is 90 mcg/day for women and 120 mcg/day for men, based on K1 [R39].

### 14.5 What to expect

| Timeframe | Change |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 weeks | 25(OH)D begins to rise; no perceptible effect |
| 8–12 weeks | 25(OH)D reaches a new steady state on a given dose |
| 3–6 months | Bone turnover markers change if deficient |
| 1–3 years | BMD changes, if any, are small and slow |
| Never | An acute "boost" in energy, immunity, or mood from a supplement in a replete person |

## 15. Product quality, brands, and cost

### 15.1 What independent testing finds

Vitamin D and K supplements are generally better than the worst categories of
supplements, but not uniformly accurate.

- **ConsumerLab (2024)** tested 26 vitamin D products and found two containing up
  to **twice** the labelled amount — enough to exceed the tolerable upper intake
  level at the labelled serving [R46].
- **JAMA Internal Medicine (2013)** analysed 55 bottles and found potencies from
  **52% to 135%** of the expected dose. Two-thirds of bottles met USP content
  standards on average, and the single USP-verified manufacturer measured
  **101.7%** [R48; R63].
- **ConsumerLab (2024, vitamin K)** found most products met claims, but one
  contained **80.8%** of its stated MK-7, and one combination contained far more
  vitamin D than listed [R47].
- **A 2023 analytical study** of 25 combined D3/K2 products reported significant
  deviations from labelled content for both vitamins, concluding that quality
  control was insufficient for most products tested [R49].
- **MK-7 isomer analysis** found all-trans content often below label and
  cis-isomer impurities in synthetic products [R45].

### 15.2 What certifications mean

| Mark | What it verifies | What it does not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **USP Verified** | Identity, potency, purity, dissolution, GMP manufacturing | Efficacy |
| **NSF/ANSI 173** | Label claims, contaminants, toxicology review, GMP | Efficacy |
| **NSF Certified for Sport** | NSF/ANSI 173 plus screening for banned substances | Efficacy |
| **Informed Sport / Informed Choice** | Banned-substance testing (batch or retail) | Potency or efficacy |
| **"Third-party tested" (unqualified)** | Nothing verifiable unless a laboratory or listing is named | Anything |

A seal is a quality hedge, not a therapeutic one. It is most valuable for
products where overdosing is a real risk (high-dose D3) or where the active
ingredient is expensive and prone to substitution (MK-7).

Two cost traps are worth naming:

   30-count bottle; buying a larger pack or a budget brand reduces this by an
   order of magnitude.

   correction dose sold as a maintenance dose, and it costs more per year at the
   reference regimen because you use fewer servings per bottle but pay for
   overage.

### 15.4 Buying guidance

- **D3:** 1000–2000 IU per capsule, third-party certified if available, large
  pack, oil-based softgel or plain capsule. Expect $20–50 per year.
- **K2:** if you choose to take it, MK-7 90–180 mcg from a product declaring
  all-trans content or using a branded ingredient (MenaQ7), with certification if
  available. Expect $20–65 per year.
- **Combinations:** convenient but rarely cost-effective and never
  evidence-superior. If you want both, buy them separately at the doses you want.
- **Avoid:** gummies for precision dosing, "high-potency" 10,000 IU products for
  maintenance, proprietary blends, and any product making cardiovascular claims.

## 17. Evidence gaps

1. **No fracture-powered K2 trial.** The largest MK-7 bone trials measured BMD,
   not fractures, over 1–3 years.
2. **No D3-versus-D3+K2 hard-outcome trial.** The combination hypothesis has never
   been tested where it matters.
3. **No long-term K2 safety data beyond three years** at supplement doses.
4. **Assay standardisation.** 25(OH)D results vary between methods and
   laboratories, complicating thresholds and meta-analyses.
5. **Obesity and dark skin dosing.** Most trials enrolled predominantly white,
   normal-weight participants; dose-response in other groups is extrapolated.
6. **Institutionalised and frail populations.** Under-represented in modern
   megatrials, despite being the group most likely to benefit.
7. **CKD and dialysis.** K2 and vitamin D analogues are widely used with limited
   outcome evidence.
8. **Publication bias.** The respiratory infection literature shows measurable
   funnel-plot asymmetry [R14], and many supplement trials are small and
   industry-funded.

## 18. Limitations of this review

- Single-reviewer, AI-assisted extraction; no duplicate independent screening.
- Paywalled independent testing (ConsumerLab) is summarised from public releases,
  limiting product-level specificity.
- Prices are a dated snapshot from brand stores and one price tracker, not a live
  feed; several were promotional.
- The product sample is purposive, not random, and does not establish market-wide
  failure rates.
- No new laboratory testing was commissioned.
- Effect estimates are best current approximations, not settled truths.

## 19. Conclusion

Vitamin D3 is a nutrient with a clear deficiency disease, a modest set of
supplement-level benefits in older and deficient populations, and a marketing
apparatus that vastly outruns its evidence. It is cheap, safe at ordinary doses,
and worth taking for many older adults. It is not a preventive for cancer, heart
disease, or infection in the well-nourished.

Vitamin K2 is a real molecule with a real mechanism and a thin clinical file. It
may slow bone loss slightly. It does not reliably slow vascular calcification, and
it has never been shown to prevent a fracture or a heart attack. Its one
unambiguous clinical interaction is dangerous: it undermines warfarin.

The combination is a product category, not an evidence-based therapy. If you want
vitamin D, buy vitamin D. If you have osteoporosis, talk to a clinician about
treatment that has been shown to reduce fractures. If you take warfarin, do not
take K2 without asking.
