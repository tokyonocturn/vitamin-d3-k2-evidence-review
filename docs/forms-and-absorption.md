# Forms, absorption and bioavailability

Most of the money in the supplement aisle is spent on form claims: "micronised",
"liposomal", "sublingual", "naturally fermented", "MK-7 from natto". Some of these
distinctions are real. Most are not worth paying for.

---

## 1. Vitamin D forms

### 1.1 D3 (cholecalciferol)

The form produced in human skin and used in nearly all clinical trials. Commercial
D3 is made by UV-irradiating 7-dehydrocholesterol from lanolin (sheep wool), or
from lichen for vegan products.

**Advantages:** best-validated; raises 25(OH)D more than D2; cheap; stable in
softgels and capsules.

**Disadvantages:** lanolin-derived products are not vegan; very high doses are
sold as "extra strength" and are easy to overuse.

### 1.2 D2 (ergocalciferol)

Made by UV-irradiating ergosterol from yeast. Used in prescription high-dose
products in the United States and in some fortified foods.

**The evidence:** a 2024 meta-analysis of 20 comparative studies found D2 produced
total 25(OH)D about **10.39 nmol/L (40%) lower** than D3 at the same daily dose,
measured by LC-MS/MS [R43]. D2 also raises 25(OH)D2 specifically, which some
immunoassays detect poorly, inflating the apparent difference in older studies.

**Verdict:** acceptable if prescribed, inferior for supplementation.

### 1.3 Calcifediol (25(OH)D3)

A synthetic form of the circulating metabolite. Per microgram it is roughly three
to five times as potent as D3 [R40]. Used clinically in some countries for rapid
correction, especially in kidney disease or malabsorption. Not a typical retail
supplement, and not interchangeable with D3 by mass.

### 1.4 Activated analogues (calcitriol, alfacalcidol)

Prescription drugs that bypass or replace the final hydroxylation. Used in CKD,
hypoparathyroidism, and some osteoporosis regimens. They carry a much higher
hypercalcemia risk than cholecalciferol and require monitoring.

**Important:** the only positive D3+K2 bone trial used **alfacalcidol**, not
cholecalciferol [R28]. Marketing that cites it as evidence for supplement
combinations is misrepresenting the study.

## 2. Vitamin K forms

### 2.1 K1 (phylloquinone)

The plant form, abundant in leafy greens. Absorbed with fat, taken up
preferentially by the liver, half-life 1–2 hours. It is the form relevant to
clotting factor synthesis and to warfarin interactions.

Retail K1 supplements exist but are not the form marketed for bone or vascular
health.

### 2.2 MK-4 (menaquinone-4)

Short-chain menaquinone found in animal products, fermented foods, and produced
synthetically. Half-life about one hour. A 420 mcg oral dose was **undetectable in
serum at every time point** in a direct comparison with MK-7, and repeated dosing
did not raise serum MK-4 [R44].

Japanese trials used **45 mg/day** — a prescription drug dose (menatetrenone) — and
reported fracture reductions in osteoporosis [R29]. Retail MK-4 products contain
45 mcg to 15 mg, which is 1000-fold below the trial dose in some cases.

**Verdict:** MK-4 is not interchangeable with MK-7, and most retail MK-4 doses are
not supported by the trials that made MK-4 famous.

### 2.3 MK-7 (menaquinone-7)

Long-chain menaquinone produced by *Bacillus subtilis* fermentation, notably in
natto. Half-life **68–72 hours**, detectable in serum for days, accumulates to
steady state over about two weeks [R59; R38]. Bioavailability is roughly 2.5× K1
at 24 hours and 6× at 96 hours [R59].

This is the form used in almost all modern bone and vascular trials, at doses from
45 to 720 mcg/day.

**Quality issue:** the active isomer is *all-trans* MK-7. Synthetic MK-7 contains
cis isomers that lack activity. A 2018 analysis found all-trans content often below
label and cis/trans impurities of 71–219 mcg per unit, with tablets tending to
under-deliver and oil capsules to over-deliver [R45]. Fermented MK-7 (MenaQ7 and
similar) avoids synthetic isomer contamination.

### 2.4 K2 as natto

Natto is the richest dietary source of MK-7. It is also a fermented soy food with a
strong taste and a large vitamin K dose, which makes it problematic for anyone on
warfarin. Dietary K2 intake has not been shown to change clinical outcomes [R39;
R59].

## 3. Absorption

### 3.1 The fat effect

Vitamin D and K are fat-soluble and are absorbed in chylomicrons. Fat stimulates
bile and pancreatic lipase, which are required for efficient uptake.

- Peak plasma vitamin D3 was **32% higher (11–52%)** with a fat-containing meal
  than a fat-free one [R41].
- A second trial found higher absorption with a low-fat meal than a high-fat meal
  or no meal, but **no difference in 25(OH)D at 30 or 90 days** [R42].

**Practical conclusion:** take with a meal containing some fat, but do not expect
a large change in your blood level. The acute pharmacokinetic effect is bigger
than the steady-state effect.

### 3.2 Vehicle: oil, powder, capsule, liquid

- For most people, oil-based softgels and dry capsules produce similar 25(OH)D
  responses.
- In fat malabsorption (cystic fibrosis, pancreatic insufficiency, cholestasis,
  bariatric surgery), vehicle and dose matter more, and powder formulations may
  outperform oil in some patients. These cases need specialist input rather than
  consumer optimisation.
- Liquid drops are convenient for children and dose titration but are easy to
  over-measure.

### 3.3 What does not have good evidence

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Sublingual absorption bypasses the need for fat | Unsupported; no 25(OH)D superiority shown |
| Liposomal D3 is substantially better absorbed | Unsupported in humans at supplement doses |
| Micronised D3 is more potent | Unsupported; particle size affects dissolution, not total absorption |
| Sprays are better than capsules | Unsupported |
| "Whole food" D3 is better absorbed | Unsupported; the molecule is the same |
| Vitamin D from mushrooms (D2) is equivalent | Inferior form, though acceptable |
| K2 from natto is better than fermented MK-7 in a capsule | Unlikely to differ; both are MK-7 |

### 3.4 Storage and stability

- Vitamin D3 in softgels is stable for years at room temperature.
- Avoid prolonged heat and direct sunlight.
- Liquid formulations may degrade faster; check expiry and store as directed.
- Gummies are more prone to potency drift and moisture uptake.

## 4. Reading a label

For vitamin D:

1. **Check IU and mcg.** 1 mcg = 40 IU. A label saying "125 mcg" is 5000 IU.
2. **Check the serving size.** "2 softgels" is common for high-potency products.
3. **Check the form.** D3 (cholecalciferol) preferred; D2 (ergocalciferol) inferior.
4. **Check certification.** USP Verified or NSF/ANSI 173 where possible.

For vitamin K:

1. **Check which menaquinone.** MK-7 and MK-4 are different drugs in practice.
2. **Check mcg.** MK-7 is dosed in micrograms; MK-4 in milligrams.
3. **Check all-trans content** if declared, or the branded ingredient (MenaQ7).
4. **Check for K1** in "K complex" products, which matters for warfarin.

For combinations:

1. **Compute the dose you would actually take.** A 5000 IU D3 + 100 mcg K2 combo
   is a correction dose of D3, not a maintenance dose.
2. **Compare the cost of the components separately.** Combinations usually cost
   more per effective dose than buying D3 and K2 separately.

---

## 5. Summary

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| D3 or D2? | D3 |
| Lanolin or lichen? | Either; lichen if vegan |
| Softgel, capsule, tablet, or liquid? | Whichever you will take consistently |
| With food? | Yes, with a meal containing fat |
| MK-4 or MK-7? | MK-7 if you want the form used in modern trials |
| Does form explain most of the price difference? | No; packaging, brand, and dose do |
| Is there a form that removes the need for warfarin caution? | No |
