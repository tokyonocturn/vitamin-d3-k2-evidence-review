# Practical safety guide

This is a decision aid, not medical advice. It assumes you are an adult
considering an over-the-counter vitamin D3 and/or K2 supplement, and it flags the
situations where self-supplementation is a bad idea.

---

## 1. The one-minute version

**Vitamin D3 is safe for most adults at 600–2000 IU/day.** The tolerable upper
intake level is 4000 IU/day. Toxicity is rare and almost always follows sustained
intakes above 10,000 IU/day.

**Vitamin K2 is safe for most adults at supplement doses**, with one absolute
exception: **it interferes with warfarin and similar anticoagulants and must not be
started without the prescribing clinician.**

**Stop and ask a clinician if you have:** kidney disease, a kidney stone history,
high blood calcium, sarcoidosis or another granulomatous disease, lymphoma,
primary hyperparathyroidism, or you take warfarin, digoxin, or thiazide diuretics.

## 3. Vitamin D3: overdose and toxicity

### 3.1 What causes it

- Sustained oral intake above 10,000 IU/day for months, usually from supplements.
- Manufacturing errors (a product containing 2–10× its label claim).
- Excessive fortification, especially in children.
- Not sun exposure. The skin limits cholecalciferol production, and excess is
  degraded to inactive metabolites.

### 3.2 What it does

Vitamin D raises intestinal calcium absorption. At toxic levels this produces:

- **Hypercalcemia** — nausea, vomiting, constipation, anorexia, abdominal pain,
  thirst, frequent urination, muscle weakness, bone pain, confusion, and in severe
  cases arrhythmia and coma.
- **Hypercalciuria** — calcium in urine, which can cause stones and, over time,
  nephrocalcinosis.
- **Soft-tissue calcification** — kidneys, blood vessels, lungs, heart.
- **Renal failure** in severe cases.

### 3.3 Thresholds

| Measure | Threshold | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 25(OH)D | <100 ng/mL | Unlikely to cause toxicity |
| 25(OH)D | 100–150 ng/mL | High; reduce dose |
| 25(OH)D | >150 ng/mL (375 nmol/L) | Consistent with toxicity if calcium is high |
| Serum calcium | >10.5 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) | Hypercalcemia; investigate |
| 24-hour urine calcium | >300 mg/day (women), >350 mg/day (men) | Hypercalciuria |

A three-year trial at 400, 4000, and 10,000 IU/day found mild hypercalcemia in 0%,
3%, and 9% of participants respectively, all reversible, with no excess of
clinical adverse events [R54]. A meta-analysis at 3200–4000 IU/day found
hypercalcemia RR 2.21 and a small increase in falls and hospitalisation [R52].

### 3.4 What to do if you suspect toxicity

1. Stop the supplement.
2. Stop calcium-containing supplements and high-calcium antacids.
3. Drink water; avoid dehydration.
4. See a clinician the same day for serum calcium, 25(OH)D, creatinine, and
   electrolytes.
5. Do not attempt to "flush" it out or take additional vitamins.
6. Severe symptoms (confusion, vomiting, palpitations, reduced urine output) are
   an emergency.

Treatment is supportive: hydration, stopping intake, and in severe cases
glucocorticoids, calcitonin, or bisphosphonates under specialist care.

## 4. Kidney stones

The relationship is widely misunderstood.

- **Vitamin D alone** at 100,000 IU monthly for a median of 3.3 years did not
  increase stone events (HR 0.90, 0.66–1.23) [R53].
- **Intermittent high-dose vitamin D** across trials showed RR 1.00 (0.84–1.19)
  for nephrolithiasis [R02].
- **Calcium supplements** are the form associated with increased stone risk,
  particularly in the WHI calcium-plus-vitamin D arm [R40].

If you have had calcium stones:

- Do not avoid vitamin D out of fear; discuss dose with a clinician.
- Avoid calcium supplements unless prescribed.
- Keep urine output high; consider citrate if advised.
- Do not take high-dose vitamin D without monitoring.

## 5. Medication interactions

### 5.1 Warfarin and other vitamin K antagonists

**This is the interaction that matters most.**

Warfarin, acenocoumarol, phenprocoumon, and tioclomarol block vitamin K recycling.
Vitamin K2 (and K1) therefore reduce their anticoagulant effect.

In a controlled dose-response study, MK-7 **45 mcg/day lowered INR by about 20%
after one week and 37% after two weeks**. Even 10–20 mcg/day was judged clinically
relevant in a substantial proportion of participants [R37].

Practical rules:

- Do not start, stop, or change a vitamin K2 supplement without telling the
  anticoagulation clinic.
- Avoid multivitamins containing vitamin K unless the clinic approves.
- Keep dietary vitamin K consistent rather than avoiding it.
- Expect more frequent INR checks after any change.

### 5.2 Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)

Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, and dabigatran do not depend on vitamin K. There
is no mechanism for K2 to reduce their effect, and no trial has tested it. This is
a "probably safe, not studied" situation, not a guarantee [R39].

### 5.3 Other drugs

| Drug | Interaction | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thiazide diuretics | Reduced calcium excretion; additive hypercalcemia | Monitor calcium at doses >2000 IU |
| Digoxin | Hypercalcemia increases toxicity risk | Keep calcium normal |
| Anticonvulsants | Lower 25(OH)D via enzyme induction | Higher maintenance dose, monitor |
| Glucocorticoids | Lower calcium absorption, bone loss | Ensure adequacy; bone protection |
| Orlistat, cholestyramine | Reduced fat-soluble vitamin absorption | Separate doses; monitor |
| Ketoconazole | Inhibits vitamin D hydroxylation | Clinician oversight |
| Aluminium antacids (dialysis) | Hypercalcemia increases aluminium absorption | Avoid if calcium is high |

## 6. Monitoring

### 6.1 Who should be monitored

- Anyone on high-dose correction (>4000 IU/day).
- Chronic kidney disease, especially dialysis.
- Sarcoidosis or other granulomatous disease.
- Primary hyperparathyroidism.
- Malabsorption or bariatric surgery.
- Long-term anticonvulsants or glucocorticoids.
- Vitamin K antagonists if K2 is being considered.
- Infants on fortified formula plus supplements.

### 6.2 What to measure and when

| Test | Baseline | 3 months | Annually |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 25(OH)D | Yes | Yes during correction | Yes |
| Serum calcium | If high dose or risk | Yes | Yes |
| Creatinine/eGFR | If kidney risk | Yes | Yes |
| PTH | If bone disease | As indicated | As indicated |
| 24-hour urine calcium | If stone history or high dose | As indicated | As indicated |
| INR | — | Within 1 week of any K2 change | Per clinic |

For most healthy adults taking 600–2000 IU/day, no monitoring is required.

## 7. Product safety

- **Check the label dose, not the bottle name.** "Extra strength" products are
  often 5000–10,000 IU, which is a correction dose.
- **Prefer certified products.** USP Verified or NSF/ANSI 173 reduce the risk of
  over- or under-dosing [R50; R51].
- **Avoid gummies for precision.** Independent testing has found potency problems
  in chewable formats, and two of 26 vitamin D products contained nearly twice
  their label dose [R46].
- **Do not stack products.** A multivitamin, a D3 capsule, and a D3+K2 combo can
  easily total 6000–8000 IU/day without anyone intending it.
- **Check for recalls.** The FDA maintains a recall database; compounding
  pharmacies and imported products have been responsible for most severe
  over-potency incidents.
- **Store properly.** Cool, dry, away from light; liquids in the refrigerator if
  the label says so.

## 8. Special populations

### Pregnancy

600 IU/day is the RDA. The 2024 Endocrine Society suggests empiric supplementation
during pregnancy but explicitly recommends against routine 25(OH)D testing in
generally healthy pregnant people [R02]. Do not exceed 4000 IU/day without
clinician involvement, and avoid single large boluses.

### Breastfeeding

Breast milk contains little vitamin D. Exclusively breastfed infants need
**400 IU/day** regardless of maternal intake.

### Infants and children

- 400 IU/day for infants.
- 600 IU/day for children and adolescents.
- Do not use adult drops without measuring; infant overdose has occurred from
  concentrated formulations.

### Older adults

- 800–1000 IU/day is the sweet spot.
- Avoid bolus dosing; it increases falls and fractures [R17].
- Check calcium and kidney function before high-dose regimens.

### Chronic kidney disease

Activation of vitamin D requires functioning kidneys. Patients may need active
analogues (calcitriol, alfacalcidol) rather than cholecalciferol, and must be
monitored for calcium and phosphate. Do not self-manage [R40].

---

## 9. When to seek urgent care

- Confusion, severe vomiting, or abdominal pain while taking high-dose vitamin D.
- Palpitations, syncope, or chest pain.
- Reduced urine output or swelling.
- Bleeding or unusual bruising after starting K2 while on warfarin.
- Any INR result outside the target range after a supplement change.

---

## 10. Summary rules

1. **D3 600–2000 IU/day** is the sensible range for most adults who supplement.
2. **Do not exceed 4000 IU/day** without a clinical reason and monitoring.
3. **Take it daily, not in annual boluses.**
4. **Do not take K2 with warfarin.**
5. **Buy certified products, and check the dose on the label.**
6. **Test only when the result will change what you do.**
7. **K2 is optional; D3 is the nutrient with the evidence.**
